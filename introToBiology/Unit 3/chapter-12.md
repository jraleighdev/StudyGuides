## Mendel's experiments and heredity

Q: In pea plants, round peas (R) are dominant to wrinkled peas (r). You do a test cross between a pea plant with wrinkled peas (genotype rr) and a plant of unknown genotype that has round peas. You end up with three plants, all of which have round peas. From this data, can you tell if the round pea parent plant is homozygous dominant or heterozygous? If the round pea parent plant is heterozygous, what is the probability that a random sample of 3 progeny peas will all be round?

A: No — the data set is too small to distinguish RR from Rr, because three round offspring can arise from a heterozygote by random chance (only a wrinkled rr offspring would prove heterozygosity). If the round parent is Rr, each offspring has a 1/2 chance of being round, so the probability that all three progeny are round is (1/2)³ = 1/8 (12.5%).

Why: A test cross is a probabilistic readout, not a proof: Rr × rr gives each offspring an independent 1/2 chance of being round, so any number of dominant offspring can occur by chance ((1/2)ⁿ for n offspring) — only a wrinkled offspring is conclusive. Genetic conclusions strengthen with sample size.

Q: In the alkaptonuria pedigree (Figure 12.6), what are the genotypes of the individuals labeled 1, 2, and 3?

A: Individual 1 = aa; individuals 2 and 3 = Aa. In this recessive-disorder pedigree, affected (blue) individuals are aa — individual 1 is affected. Individuals 2 and 3 are both unaffected, yet each has an affected (aa) child, and an aa child must receive a recessive allele from each parent, so each must be a heterozygous carrier. (This is the caption's principle: a person's genotype can often be deduced from their offspring; individuals with no affected offspring are the ones marked "A?".)

Why: Recessive alleles hide in carriers, so pedigrees are read from affected individuals upward: an aa child must have gotten one recessive allele from *each* parent, which pins any unaffected parent of an affected child as an obligate carrier (Aa). Affected individuals are unambiguously aa; unaffected people with no affected children remain unknown ("A?").

Q: In Drosophila (Figure 12.12), what ratio of offspring would result from a cross between a white-eyed male and a female that is heterozygous for red eye color?

A: 1:1:1:1 — one red-eyed female (XW Xw) : one white-eyed female (Xw Xw) : one red-eyed male (XW Y) : one white-eyed male (Xw Y) — because the father passes his only X to all daughters and his Y to all sons, while the heterozygous mother passes XW or Xw to offspring of both sexes at random, so each sex splits half red, half white.

Why: An X-linked gene rides the sex chromosomes, and those segregate like any homologous pair: the father's lone X must go to every daughter and his Y to every son, while the heterozygous mother's XW or Xw sorts at random to both sexes. Two independent 1:1 splits (sex × eye allele) multiply into the 1:1:1:1 ratio.

Q: In pea plants, round seed shape (R) is dominant to wrinkled seed shape (r) and yellow peas (Y) are dominant to green peas (y). What are the possible genotypes and phenotypes for a cross between RrYY and rrYy pea plants? How many squares do you need to do a Punnett square analysis of this cross?

A: Genotypes: RrYY, RrYy, rrYY, and rrYy in a 1:1:1:1 ratio. Phenotypes: 1 round yellow : 1 wrinkled yellow — every offspring is yellow (both parents carry a Y allele), so only seed shape segregates. You need only a 2 × 2 Punnett square (4 squares), because RrYY produces just RY and rY gametes and rrYy produces just rY and ry — each parent is homozygous at one of the two genes.

Why: A Punnett square needs only one column/row per distinct gamete type each parent can make, and homozygous genes contribute just one allele — RrYY makes only RY and rY, rrYy only rY and ry, so 2 × 2 = 4 squares suffices. And since both parents carry a Y, yellow is fixed and only the R gene segregates.

### Chapter 12 Review Questions (OpenStax Biology 2e)

Q5: Mendel performed hybridizations by transferring pollen from the _____ of the male plant to the female ova.

A: The anther — the male organ where pollen is produced. (The pistil and stigma are female structures; pollen is transferred onto the stigma.)

Why: In flowering plants the male gametes travel inside pollen grains, which are produced in the anther; fertilization happens when pollen lands on the stigma and grows a tube down to the ovum. Hybridization is just controlled pollen transfer from anther to stigma.

Q6: Which is one of the seven characteristics that Mendel observed in pea plants?

A: Seed texture — Mendel's seven were seed shape (texture), seed color, flower color, flower position, pod shape, pod color, and plant height.

Why: Mendel deliberately picked characteristics that varied in clear either/or form, were easy to score visually, and existed in true-breeding varieties — unambiguous, discrete traits are what make ratios interpretable. Seed shape (round vs. wrinkled) is one of those seven.

Q7: In a cross between true-breeding green-seed and yellow-seed pea plants (yellow dominant), what F1 offspring would you expect?

A: 100 percent yellow seeds — every offspring is a Yy heterozygote, and the dominant yellow allele masks the green allele.

Why: "True-breeding" means homozygous, so YY × yy can produce only Yy offspring — and dominance means a single Y dictates the phenotype. A uniform F1 is the signature of homozygous parents at that locus.

Q8: Inflated pods are dominant to constricted. If you obtained 650 inflated-pod plants in the F2 generation, approximately how many constricted-pod plants would you expect?

A: About 217 — the F2 of a monohybrid cross segregates 3 inflated : 1 constricted, so constricted ≈ 650 ÷ 3.

Why: The F2 of two heterozygotes follows a 3:1 phenotypic ratio, so *expected counts* scale in that proportion — divide the dominant class by 3 to get the recessive class. The ratio, not a fixed number, is what carries across experiments.

Q9: A true-breeding pea plant with violet, terminal flowers is crossed with a true-breeding plant with white, axial flowers. What would the F2 generation look like?

A: 75% violet flowers; 75% axial flowers — the F1 are all VvAa (violet and axial, both dominant), and the F2 self-cross gives an independent 3:1 ratio for each trait.

Why: Two true-breeding parents make a uniform double-heterozygote F1; the F1 self-cross then segregates each gene independently at 3:1, and 3/4 × 3/4 predicts any trait combination. Independent genes are handled one ratio at a time, then multiplied.

Q10: The observable traits expressed by an organism are described as its _____.

A: Phenotype — the genotype is the underlying allele makeup, including alleles that aren't visible.

Why: The distinction is observable vs. underlying: phenotype is what you can see or measure; genotype is the full allele set — including recessive alleles a dominant phenotype hides.

Q11: A recessive trait will be observed in individuals that are _____ for that trait.

A: Homozygous — two recessive alleles are needed since a dominant allele would mask it.

Why: Dominance is masking: one dominant allele dictates the phenotype, so a recessive trait can only appear when *no* dominant allele is present to mask it — which requires two recessive alleles.

Q12: If black and white true-breeding mice mate and all offspring are gray, what inheritance pattern is this?

A: Incomplete dominance — the heterozygote's phenotype (gray) is intermediate between the two homozygotes.

Why: In incomplete dominance neither allele fully masks the other, so the heterozygote's appearance is a blend of the two homozygotes — the gray F1 halfway between black and white. A heterozygote with its own distinct phenotype is the tell (versus a dominant F1).

Q13: ABO blood groups (IA, IB, i alleles; one quarter of IAi × IBi offspring are IAIB with both antigens expressed equally) are an example of _____.

A: Multiple alleles and codominance — three alleles exist in the population (multiple alleles), and in IAIB heterozygotes both A and B antigens are expressed equally (codominance).

Why: Two separate concepts stack here: more than two alleles exist *in the population* (multiple alleles — any individual still carries only two), and the IAIB heterozygote expresses both gene products fully and simultaneously (codominance — unlike the blending of incomplete dominance).

Q14: In a mating between two heterozygotes for a recessive lethal allele expressed in utero, what genotypic ratio (homozygous dominant : heterozygous : homozygous recessive) would be observed?

A: 1:2:0 — the homozygous recessive class dies before birth, so among live offspring only AA and Aa remain (a 2:1 ratio).

Why: A recessive lethal allele removes the aa class before it can be counted, so it vanishes from the observed ratio — but segregation still happened normally at conception (1:2:1). A missing genotypic class among liveborn offspring is itself evidence of lethality.

Q15: If the allele encoding polydactyly (six fingers) is dominant, why do most people have five fingers?

A: The polydactyl allele is very rare in the human population — dominant does not mean common; how often a trait appears depends on allele frequency.

Why: Dominance describes how alleles interact within a genotype, not how frequent they are in a population — a dominant allele can be rare and a recessive one common. Trait frequency is set by allele frequencies, so a dominant trait can still be unusual.

Q16: Black and white chickens produce all speckled offspring. What should the farmer expect when the speckled chickens are interbred?

A: 50% speckled, 25% black, and 25% white — the speckled birds are heterozygotes expressing both alleles (codominance), so their cross yields a 1:2:1 phenotype ratio.

Why: Codominance makes the heterozygote its own distinct third phenotype, so the usual 1:2:1 genotype ratio shows up as three phenotype classes instead of two — and the heterozygote class (speckled) is twice as common as either homozygote.

Q17: Assuming no gene linkage, in a dihybrid cross of AABB × aabb with AaBb F1 heterozygotes, what is the ratio of the F1 gametes (AB, aB, Ab, ab) that will give rise to the F2 offspring?

A: 1:1:1:1 — with independent assortment, each heterozygous gene pair sorts its alleles independently, making all four gamete types equally likely.

Why: With no linkage, each heterozygous locus contributes its dominant or recessive allele with equal probability, and every combination across loci is equally likely — 2 genes × 2 equally likely alleles each = 4 equal gamete classes (2ⁿ for n heterozygous genes).

Q18: The forked line and probability methods make use of what probability rule?

A: The product rule — both methods multiply the probabilities of the independent monohybrid outcomes along each path.

Why: Both shortcuts decompose a multi-gene cross into independent single-gene events and multiply probabilities along each branch path: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) for independent events — that's the product rule.

Q19: How many different offspring genotypes and phenotypes are expected in a trihybrid cross between parents heterozygous all three traits (dominant/recessive pattern)?

A: 27 genotypes; 8 phenotypes — genotypes = 3³ (each gene contributes AA, Aa, or aa), phenotypes = 2³ (each gene contributes a dominant or recessive phenotype).

Why: Per gene, a heterozygote × heterozygote cross yields 3 genotypes but only 2 phenotype classes (dominant vs. recessive); independent genes multiply, giving 3ⁿ genotypes and 2ⁿ phenotypes for n heterozygous gene pairs.

Q20: Labrador retriever fur color: ee__ dogs are yellow, B_E_ black, bbE_ chocolate. This is an example of _____.

A: Epistasis — the E gene masks the B gene's effect: ee makes the dog yellow regardless of which B alleles it carries.

Why: In epistasis one gene's expression depends on or masks another gene's: here the E locus controls whether pigment reaches the fur at all, so ee blocks deposition and whatever B alleles are present have nothing visible to affect.

Q21: Which situation does not follow the Law of Independent Assortment?

A: Men are more likely to experience hemophilia than women — hemophilia is X-linked (sex linkage), so the trait is inherited coupled to the sex chromosome rather than assorting independently. The other options are single-gene crosses or chance outcomes, not linked inheritance.

Why: Independent assortment applies to genes on different (non-homologous) chromosome pairs; an X-linked gene is physically coupled to a sex chromosome, so its inheritance pattern is tied to sex rather than assorting freely. Sex linkage is the classic exception.

### Chapter 12 Critical Thinking Questions (OpenStax Biology 2e)

Q22: Describe one of the reasons why the garden pea was an excellent choice of model system for studying inheritance.

A: The pea is sessile and its petals stay sealed tightly until after self-pollination — this prevents accidental or unintentional fertilizations that would have compromised Mendel's data, and generations of selfing produced true-breeding lines. (It also matures in one season and produces many offspring, but the sealed-flower self-pollination is the classic answer.)

Why: To read inheritance from crosses, Mendel needed to control who mated with whom: closed flowers enforce self-pollination (no stray pollen), and repeated selfing generates true-breeding homozygous lines whose crosses give predictable, interpretable ratios. Control over mating is what makes a genetics model organism.

Q23: How would you perform a reciprocal cross for the characteristic of stem height in the garden pea?

A: Two paired crosses with the sex roles reversed: (1) pollen from a true-breeding tall plant onto the stigma of a true-breeding dwarf plant, and (2) pollen from a true-breeding dwarf plant onto the stigma of a true-breeding tall plant. Compare the F1 and F2 of each cross to see whether the outcome depends on which parent donated which trait (for stem height, it doesn't).

Why: A reciprocal cross pair tests whether the outcome depends on which *sex* contributes which trait: matching results in both directions indicate an autosomal trait, while a difference would implicate sex linkage or a maternal/cytoplasmic effect. Stem height shows no such difference.

Q24: Cross a true-breeding round, yellow seed plant with a true-breeding green, wrinkled seed plant. What is the probability offspring will have green, round seeds — F1 and F2?

A: F1 = 0 (all offspring are RrYy — round, yellow; the recessive green allele is masked). F2 = 3/16 (18.75%): green requires rr (1/4), round requires R_ (3/4), and the product rule gives 1/4 × 3/4 = 3/16.

Why: True-breeding parents make every F1 a double heterozygote, so recessive phenotypes can't appear in F1. In the F2 self-cross the two genes segregate independently — P(rr) = 1/4 and P(R_) = 3/4 — and the product rule multiplies them.

Q25: Calculate the probability of selecting a heart or a face card when heart suit face cards are excluded from a standard deck. Is this more or less likely than selecting a heart suit face card?

A: 22/52 = 11/26 ≈ 42% — hearts excluding face cards (13 − 3 = 10) plus face cards (12), summed by the sum rule since they're now mutually exclusive. This is far more likely than drawing a heart suit face card, which is 3/52 ≈ 6%.

Why: For mutually exclusive events the sum rule adds probabilities — but first remove the overlap (the 3 heart face cards) so the two sets don't double-count. Counting favorable outcomes over the 52-card total: 10 + 12 = 22.

Q26: Axial (A) is dominant to terminal (a) for flower position. List all possible F1 and F2 genotypes and phenotypes from a cross of homozygous parents.

A: Parents AA (axial) × aa (terminal). F1: all Aa, axial phenotype. F2 (Aa × Aa): AA : Aa : aa = 1:2:1 — AA and Aa are axial, aa is terminal, so a 3 axial : 1 terminal phenotypic ratio.

Why: Homozygous parents make a uniform heterozygous F1; the F1 self-cross segregates alleles 1:2:1 by genotype, which dominance collapses to 3:1 by phenotype. This is the standard monohybrid pattern — every Mendelian cross question reduces to it.

Q27: Cross a dwarf pea plant (homozygous recessive) with a tall pea plant (heterozygous). What is the phenotypic ratio?

A: tt × Tt gives Tt, Tt, tt, tt — a 1 tall : 1 dwarf phenotypic ratio.

Why: Crossing to a homozygous recessive (a test cross) exposes the heterozygote's 1:1 gamete ratio directly — the recessive parent contributes only recessive alleles, so each offspring's phenotype reveals exactly which allele came from the heterozygote.

Q28: Can a human male be a carrier of red-green color blindness?

A: No — males have only one X chromosome (hemizygous), so a male carrying the color-blindness allele always expresses it. Being a carrier requires two X chromosomes: one with the normal allele to mask the defective one.

Why: Males are hemizygous for X-linked genes — one X, no second allele — so a recessive X allele is always expressed, never silently carried. "Carrier" (has the allele, doesn't express it) requires a second X with the dominant allele to mask it.

Q29: Why is it more efficient to perform a test cross with a homozygous recessive donor than a homozygous dominant donor? How could the same information be found with a homozygous dominant donor?

A: An rr donor contributes only recessive alleles, so offspring phenotypes directly expose the unknown parent's gametes — any recessive offspring proves the unknown is Rr; the genotype is read in one generation. With an RR donor every offspring receives a dominant allele and shows the dominant phenotype regardless, so you'd have to self-cross the F1 and read the unknown parent's genotype from the F2 segregation ratios — an extra generation.

Why: A homozygous recessive donor contributes only recessive alleles, so each offspring's phenotype *is* the unknown parent's gamete — one generation, genotype read directly. A homozygous dominant donor masks everything, forcing an F2 segregation analysis to recover the same information.

Q30: Use the probability method to calculate the genotypes and genotypic proportions of a cross between AABBCc and Aabbcc parents.

A: Gene A: AA × Aa → 1/2 AA, 1/2 Aa. Gene B: BB × bb → all Bb. Gene C: Cc × cc → 1/2 Cc, 1/2 cc. Multiplying: AABbCc, AABbcc, AaBbCc, and AaBbcc, each 1/4 (a 1:1:1:1 ratio).

Why: The probability method treats each gene as an independent monohybrid cross and multiplies outcomes along each path: A splits 1/2 : 1/2, B is fixed (all Bb), C splits 1/2 : 1/2 — so each of the four combinations lands at 1/2 × 1 × 1/2 = 1/4. No Punnett square needed; the product rule does the work.

Q31: Explain epistasis in terms of its Greek-language roots "standing upon."

A: Epistasis is an antagonistic interaction between genes where one gene masks or interferes with the expression of another — the interfering (epistatic) gene acts as if it is standing upon the other (hypostatic) gene to block its expression, like Labrador E standing on B.

Why: The etymology is the mechanism: Greek epi- ("upon") + stasis ("standing") — the epistatic gene stands upon the hypostatic one, overriding its expression, exactly as the Labrador E locus stands on B to control whether pigment appears at all.

Q32: Cross white WwYy summer squash heterozygotes to prove the 12 white : 3 yellow : 1 green phenotypic ratio.

A: The dominant W allele is epistatic: any W_ squash is white regardless of its Y alleles → P(W_) = 3/4 = 12/16 white. Only ww squash show the Y gene: wwY_ (yellow) = 1/4 × 3/4 = 3/16, and wwyy (green) = 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16. Ratio: 12:3:1.

Why: Dominant epistasis layers the ratios: W_ (3/4) masks the Y gene, and only inside the remaining ww quarter (1/4) does Y segregate 3:1 — giving 3/16 yellow and 1/16 green on top of 12/16 white. The 12:3:1 ratio is just a 3:1 ratio nested inside the fraction not masked.

Q33: People with trisomy 21 develop Down's syndrome. What law of Mendelian inheritance is violated, and what is the most likely way this occurs?

A: The law of segregation — a gamete should carry one copy of each chromosome, but here offspring inherit three copies of chromosome 21. It usually occurs when homologous chromosomes fail to separate during the first round of meiosis (meiosis I nondisjunction), producing a gamete carrying two copies of chromosome 21; fertilization by a normal gamete yields the trisomy.

Why: The law of segregation predicts each gamete carries exactly one copy of each chromosome; nondisjunction at meiosis I sends both homologs of chromosome 21 to the same pole, producing a two-copy gamete — and fertilization by a normal gamete adds the third. An extra whole chromosome is the fingerprint of a segregation failure, not a gene mutation.

Q34: A heterozygous pea plant produces violet flowers and yellow, round seeds. Describe the expected gamete genotypes under Mendelian inheritance. If all three genes are on the same arm of one chromosome, should a scientist predict Mendelian inheritance?

A: The plant is VvYyRr, so with independent assortment there are 2³ = 8 equally likely gametes: VYR, VYr, VyR, Vyr, vYR, vYr, vyR, and vyr. No — if all three genes sit close together on the same chromosome arm they are linked and will tend to be inherited together, so independent assortment would not hold and Mendelian predictions would fail.

Why: n independently assorting heterozygous genes give 2ⁿ equally likely gamete types (2³ = 8 here). But independent assortment requires genes on different chromosomes (or far apart); genes close together on one arm are physically linked and travel together, so Mendelian ratios break down.
