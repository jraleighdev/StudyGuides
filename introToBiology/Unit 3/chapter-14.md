## DNA Structure and function

Q: In eukaryotic cells, DNA and RNA synthesis occur in a separate compartment from protein synthesis. In prokaryotic cells, both processes occur together. What advantages might there be to separating the processes? What advantages might there be to having them occur together?

A: Separating them (nucleus vs. cytoplasm) lets a eukaryotic cell divide gene expression into discrete steps — the RNA can be capped, spliced, and poly-adenylated before it's ever translated — which allows more complex RNA and protein products and more points of regulation. Having them together in prokaryotes is fast: translation begins before transcription even finishes, so proteins appear much more quickly.

Why: A physical boundary between transcription and translation inserts a processing stage — capping, splicing, polyadenylation — each a point for regulation and for building more complex transcripts, at the cost of speed. Coupling them removes the delay entirely: ribosomes start translating the 5′ end of an mRNA while its 3′ end is still being transcribed. Complexity vs. speed is the trade.

Q: You isolate a cell strain in which the joining of Okazaki fragments is impaired and suspect that a mutation has occurred in an enzyme found at the replication fork. Which enzyme is most likely to be mutated?

A: DNA ligase — it seals the nicks between Okazaki fragments, catalyzing the phosphodiester bond between the 3′-OH end of one fragment and the 5′ phosphate of the next. (DNA pol I fills the gaps left by removed RNA primers, but ligase is what joins the fragments into one continuous strand.)

Why: Each replication enzyme has one job, so the symptom names the enzyme: sealing fragments into a continuous strand is ligase's exclusive role — it catalyzes the phosphodiester bond between fragments. DNA pol I fills primer gaps but cannot make the final seal, so unjoined Okazaki fragments point at ligase.

Q: A frameshift mutation that results in the insertion of three nucleotides is often less deleterious than a mutation that results in the insertion of one nucleotide. Why?

A: Three nucleotides = one whole codon. A three-base insertion just adds one extra amino acid; the reading frame stays intact, so every codon downstream still reads the same. A one-nucleotide insertion shifts the reading frame, changing every codon after the insertion point — a completely different (usually nonfunctional) protein.

Why: The code is read in non-overlapping triplets, so the reading frame only survives length changes that are multiples of three: inserting three nucleotides adds one whole codon (one extra amino acid) and leaves every downstream codon unchanged, while a single nucleotide shifts the frame of every codon after it. Frame integrity, not insertion size, determines the damage.

### Chapter 14 Review Questions (OpenStax Biology 2e)

Q4: If DNA of a particular species contains 27 percent A, what is the percentage of C?

A: 23 percent — Chargaff's rules: A = T and G = C. A + T = 54%, leaving 46% for G + C, split evenly.

Why: In double-stranded DNA every A pairs with a T and every G with a C, so the bases come in equal pairs — A% = T% and G% = C% by construction. Whatever fraction A+T don't take, G and C split evenly: 27 + 27 = 54, leaving 46, so 23 each.

Q5: Hershey and Chase confirmed DNA was the hereditary material on the finding that:

A: Radioactive phosphorus was found in the cell — they labeled phage DNA with ³²P (P is in DNA, not protein) and protein coats with ³⁵S; only the ³²P entered the bacteria.

Why: Each isotope is chemically exclusive to one macromolecule — phosphorus only in DNA's backbone, sulfur only in protein — so the label tracks exactly one molecule. Whichever isotope entered the infected cell therefore identified which molecule carried the hereditary instructions into it: only ³²P did.

Q6: Bacterial transformation is a major concern in medical settings. Why might health care providers be concerned?

A: All of the above — gene transfer can move disease-causing genes into non-pathogenic bacteria, spread antibiotic resistance (creating "superbugs"), and bacteriophages can ferry toxin-encoding DNA to new bacteria.

Why: Transformation, conjugation, and transduction all move DNA between bacteria — including virulence genes, resistance genes, and toxin genes — so any of them can turn a harmless strain into a pathogen or a treatable infection into a drug-resistant one. Horizontal gene transfer is why resistance spreads faster than mutation alone could explain.

Q7: The DNA double helix does not have which of the following?

A: Uracil — DNA uses thymine. The helix does have antiparallel strands, complementary base pairing, and major/minor grooves.

Why: DNA's bases are A, T, G, C — thymine, not uracil; uracil replaces thymine in RNA. The other listed features are genuine structural properties of the double helix, so uracil is the odd one out by chemistry.

Q8: In eukaryotes, what is the DNA wrapped around?

A: Histones — forming nucleosomes ("beads on a string"), further compacted into 30-nm fibers and metaphase chromosomes.

Why: Eukaryotic DNA's first level of compaction is wrapping ~147 bp around a histone octamer — the nucleosome — which then coils into 30-nm fibers and higher-order structures. Without histones, meters of DNA could not fit inside a micrometer-scale nucleus.

Q9: Meselson and Stahl's experiments proved that DNA replicates by which mode?

A: Semi-conservative — each daughter DNA molecule retains one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand.

Why: Of the three proposed models (conservative, semi-conservative, dispersive), only semi-conservative predicts each daughter duplex contains one old strand and one new — and that is exactly what Meselson–Stahl's density tracking showed. It is also what Watson and Crick's complementary base pairing structurally implied.

Q10: If the 5′–3′ strand is AATGCTAC, the complementary sequence is:

A: 3′–TTACGATG–5′ — complement each base (A↔T, G↔C) with antiparallel orientation.

Why: Two rules combine: complementarity pairs A with T and G with C, and antiparallel orientation means the new strand runs 3′→5′ against the 5′→3′ template — so the answer is written in reverse order relative to the given strand. Sequence questions are always complement + flip.

Q11: How did Meselson and Stahl support Watson and Crick's double-helix model?

A: They demonstrated that each strand serves as a template for synthesizing a new strand of DNA — semi-conservative replication is exactly what the complementary double helix predicts.

Why: The double helix's two strands are complementary, so each one fully specifies the other — the structure itself predicts templated replication. Meselson–Stahl's single intermediate-density band after one round confirmed each old strand remains intact and templates a new one, validating the model's built-in mechanism.

Q12: Which component is not involved during the formation of the replication fork?

A: Ligase — its job (sealing Okazaki fragments) happens behind the fork after synthesis. Helicase opens the helix to form the fork, SSB proteins coat the separated strands, and the fork forms at the origin of replication.

Why: The fork *forms* when helicase opens the helix at the origin and SSBs stabilize the separated strands; ligase's work — sealing Okazaki fragments into one continuous lagging strand — happens later, behind the fork. Enzyme location in the sequence of events determines which step it belongs to.

Q13: What does the enzyme primase synthesize?

A: An RNA primer — a short (5–10 nucleotide) stretch complementary to the template, providing the free 3′-OH that DNA polymerase needs to start.

Why: DNA polymerase can only extend an existing chain — it requires a free 3′-OH to add to and cannot start synthesis de novo. Primase solves the cold-start problem by laying down a short RNA stretch complementary to the template, providing that starting 3′-OH.

Q14: In which direction does DNA replication take place?

A: 5′–3′ — DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to a free 3′-OH end.

Why: The polymerase's chemistry is one-directional: each incoming nucleotide's 5′-phosphate is bonded to the 3′-OH of the growing chain, so chains only ever grow at the 3′ end. This single constraint is what forces the lagging strand's fragment-based synthesis.

Q15: A scientist randomly mutates a bacterium's DNA; its daughter cells show many errors in their replicated DNA. Which enzyme was likely mutated?

A: DNA pol II — it is the enzyme primarily required for DNA repair (DNA pol III, the main replication enzyme, isn't among the choices); without repair, replication errors persist and accumulate in the daughters.

Why: The bacterial polymerases are specialized: pol III is the main replication enzyme, while pol I and pol II do repair. A mutator phenotype — errors persisting into daughter DNA — points at the repair function, not the copier: replication happens fine, but nothing fixes the mistakes.

Q16: The ends of the linear chromosomes are maintained by:

A: Telomerase — it extends the 3′ chromosome ends using its built-in RNA template, solving the end-replication problem (the 5′-end primer can't be replaced with DNA).

Why: The end-replication problem: removing the last RNA primer on a linear chromosome leaves a 5′-end gap no polymerase can fill, so ends would erode every cycle. Telomerase carries its own RNA template and extends the 3′ telomeric overhang, pushing the eventual gap into expendable repeat DNA.

Q17: Which statement comparing prokaryotic and eukaryotic DNA replication is NOT true?

A: "DNA replication always occurs in the nucleus" — prokaryotes have no nucleus; their replication happens in the cytoplasm.

Why: Prokaryotes have no nucleus — their chromosome sits in the cytoplasm — so any statement placing their replication in a nucleus is false by definition. Eukaryotic replication is nuclear (plus mitochondria and chloroplasts); prokaryotic is cytoplasmic.

Q18: During proofreading, which enzyme reads the DNA?

A: DNA polymerase — its 3′→5′ exonuclease activity checks each newly added base and removes mismatches.

Why: The same enzyme that synthesizes DNA also checks it: DNA polymerase's 3′→5′ exonuclease activity backs up, excises a mispaired base, and replaces it — proofreading is built into the replication machinery rather than being a separate system.

Q19: The initial mechanism for repairing nucleotide errors in DNA is:

A: DNA polymerase proofreading — it catches errors as replication happens; mismatch repair and nucleotide excision repair are later backup systems.

Why: Repair is layered by timing: proofreading operates in real time as each nucleotide is added, catching most mismatches immediately, while mismatch repair and nucleotide excision repair are post-replication backups that sweep what slipped through. First line of defense = the enzyme making the copy.

Q20: A mutation eliminates the exonuclease function of DNA pol. What mutational load do you predict in the adult flies?

A: Significantly more mutations than average — without exonuclease (proofreading) activity, replication errors that would normally be excised and replaced persist in the DNA.

Why: Exonuclease activity *is* the proofreading function — excising wrongly incorporated bases before the next one is added. Knocking it out doesn't stop replication but removes error correction, so every mistake that would have been fixed becomes a permanent mutation copied into all daughter cells.

### Chapter 14 Critical Thinking Questions (OpenStax Biology 2e)

Q21: Explain Griffith's transformation experiments. What did he conclude from them?

A: Griffith injected mice with four forms of *Streptococcus pneumoniae*: live rough (R, harmless) cells — mice lived; live smooth (S, virulent) cells — mice died; heat-killed S cells — mice lived; and heat-killed S + live R — mice died, and live S cells were cultured from them. Conclusion: a "transforming principle" from the dead S cells had permanently converted live R cells into virulent S cells — hereditary material could move between bacteria (later identified as DNA by Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty).

Why: The logic of the four-arm experiment: dead S alone was harmless and live R alone was harmless, yet together they killed the mice and yielded live S. Something hereditary must have moved from dead S into living R — a "transforming principle" that transferred between bacteria, which Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty later identified as DNA.

Q22: Why were radioactive sulfur and phosphorous used to label bacteriophage in Hershey and Chase's experiments?

A: The two elements separate protein from DNA cleanly: phosphorus is in DNA's backbone but absent from protein, so ³²P labels only DNA; sulfur appears in protein (cysteine, methionine) but not DNA, so ³⁵S labels only protein. Tracking whichever isotope entered the infected cells showed which molecule carried the hereditary instructions — only the ³²P-labeled DNA did.

Why: A label is only interpretable if it is exclusive to one molecule, and biochemistry hands you a perfect pair: phosphorus is unique to the DNA backbone and sulfur is unique to protein amino acids. Each isotope therefore tags exactly one molecule, so "which label enters the cell" answers "which molecule carries the genes."

Q23: The tetranucleotide hypothesis said DNA was composed of repeating GACT units. How did Chargaff disprove it?

A: GACT repeats would give exactly 25% of each base in every species. Chargaff measured base composition across species and found it varies — some genomes are A+T-rich, others G+C-rich — so DNA cannot be a fixed repeating unit. His other observation, that A ≈ T and G ≈ C within a species, later became key evidence for Watson–Crick base pairing.

Why: A fixed repeating unit (GACTGACT…) would force exactly 25% of each base in every species that used it — a prediction about *ratios*. Chargaff's measurements varied between species (some AT-rich, some GC-rich), falsifying the repeating-unit structure; and within each species A = T and G = C, the clue that later became base-pairing evidence.

Q24: Provide a brief summary of the Sanger sequencing method.

A: Chain termination. Copy the target DNA from a primer using DNA polymerase, normal nucleotides, and a small fraction of dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs). A ddNTP lacks the 3′-OH, so the chain stops the moment one is incorporated. Run four reactions (one per ddNTP) — or one reaction with differently fluorescent-labeled ddNTPs in modern dye-terminator sequencing — to get a ladder of fragments, each ending at an occurrence of a particular base. Sort by length with electrophoresis and read the terminal bases in order: that ladder is the sequence.

Why: A ddNTP lacks the 3′-OH that the next phosphodiester bond requires, so its incorporation terminates the chain at exactly that base. Because termination is random per copy, the reaction generates fragments of every possible length; sorting them by size and reading each fragment's final base reconstructs the whole sequence — replication chemistry turned into a readout.

Q25: Describe the structure and complementary base pairing of DNA.

A: Two antiparallel strands form a right-handed double helix — sugar-phosphate backbones on the outside, bases stacked inside like rungs. A purine always pairs with a pyrimidine: A with T (two hydrogen bonds) and G with C (three), making the strands complementary so each can serve as the template for the other.

Why: Complementarity is the design principle: purine + pyrimidine pairs (A–T, G–C) keep the helix a constant width, and antiparallel orientation lets each strand fully specify the other. That mutual templating is precisely what makes replication, repair, and transcription possible — structure dictates function.

Q26: Prokaryotes have a single circular chromosome while eukaryotes have linear chromosomes. Describe one advantage and one disadvantage of eukaryotic genome packaging.

A: Advantage — winding linear DNA around histones packs far more DNA into the cell, so the organism can carry more information; that's what enabled multicellularity and cell specialization. Disadvantage — maintaining all that genetic material costs more energy and offers more opportunities for replication errors.

Why: Histone compaction is what lets a eukaryote carry vastly more DNA than a naked circular chromosome could hold — enabling larger genomes, multicellularity, and specialization. But size has a price: more DNA means more energy to maintain and replicate, and more base pairs mean more opportunities for replication errors. Capacity vs. cost is the trade.

Q27: How did the scientific community learn that DNA replication takes place in a semi-conservative fashion?

A: Meselson and Stahl grew E. coli for generations in ¹⁵N (heavy) medium, then shifted it to ¹⁴N (light) medium and separated the DNA by density after each generation. After one round, a single intermediate-density band appeared — each molecule had one old heavy strand and one new light strand. After two rounds, half the DNA was intermediate and half was light. Only the semi-conservative model predicts that pattern; conservative replication would keep an all-heavy band, and dispersive would give only intermediate bands that gradually lighten.

Why: ¹⁵N- and ¹⁴N-containing DNA differ in density, so a CsCl gradient physically separates heavy, hybrid, and light molecules — the isotope acts as a strand tracer. The observed pattern (one hybrid band after one round, hybrid + light after two) is uniquely predicted by each parental strand remaining intact within a new duplex; conservative would retain a heavy band, dispersive would never yield a discrete light band.

Q28: If the Meselson–Stahl experiments had supported conservative replication instead, what would you predict after two rounds of replication?

A: Two bands and never an intermediate one: the original all-¹⁵N helix stays intact — 25% of the total DNA, the lower (heavier) band — and every newly synthesized helix is all-¹⁴N — 75%, the higher (lighter) band. (After one round it would be 50% heavy / 50% light.)

Why: Conservative replication keeps the parental duplex whole and builds entirely new duplexes beside it, so DNA sorts into two discrete densities — the original all-heavy helix (1/4 of the DNA after two doublings) and all-light copies (3/4) — and an intermediate band is impossible because no molecule ever mixes old and new strands.

Q29: DNA replication is bidirectional and discontinuous; explain your understanding of those concepts.

A: Bidirectional — from a single origin, two replication forks move away from each other around the chromosome, copying the genome from both sides at once. Discontinuous — DNA polymerase only synthesizes 5′→3′ and the template strands are antiparallel, so the leading strand is copied continuously toward the fork, while the lagging strand is copied in short Okazaki fragments that start at new RNA primers as more template is exposed and are joined afterward.

Why: Bidirectional means two forks set out from each origin in opposite directions, copying both ways at once — halving genome copying time. Discontinuous is forced by chemistry and geometry: one-way 5′→3′ synthesis against antiparallel templates means only one strand (leading) can be copied toward the fork; the other (lagging) must be synthesized in short away-from-fork bursts.

Q30: What are Okazaki fragments and how are they formed?

A: Short pieces of newly synthesized DNA on the lagging strand. The lagging template is exposed running toward the fork, but polymerase can only extend away from the fork — so each time helicase opens more helix, primase lays a fresh RNA primer and DNA pol III extends it in a short burst pointing away from the fork. Each burst is one fragment; DNA pol I later replaces each primer with DNA, and ligase seals the fragments into one strand.

Why: On the lagging strand the newly exposed template runs 3′→5′ toward the fork, but polymerase's 5′→3′ chemistry forces synthesis away from the fork — so replication restarts from a fresh primer each time more single-stranded template appears. Discontinuity is a geometric necessity, not a choice; pol I and ligase clean up the seams afterward.

Q31: If the rate of replication in a particular prokaryote is 900 nucleotides per second, how long would it take 1.2 million base pair genomes to make two copies?

A: 1.2 million bp ÷ 900 nt/s ≈ 1333 s ≈ 22.2 minutes. (The official key computes one genome-length pass; bidirectional replication from a single origin would actually halve the real time to ~11 minutes.)

Why: Genome size ÷ polymerase rate gives the single-pass copying time: 1.2 × 10⁶ bp ÷ 900 bp/s ≈ 1333 s. The official key does this simple division; note that bidirectional replication from a single origin would halve the actual time — a place where the published hint simplifies.

Q32: Explain the events taking place at the replication fork. If the gene for helicase is mutated, what part of replication will be affected?

A: At the fork: helicase unwinds the double helix; single-strand binding proteins keep the separated strands stable; topoisomerase relieves the supercoiling that builds ahead; primase lays RNA primers; DNA pol III extends the leading strand continuously and the lagging strand in Okazaki fragments; DNA pol I swaps the primers for DNA; ligase seals the nicks. Without helicase the helix never opens — no template is exposed, no fork forms, and replication cannot proceed at all.

Why: Replication is an ordered assembly line — helicase opens the helix, SSB stabilizes, topoisomerase relieves torque, primase starts, pol III extends, pol I and ligase finish — and each step depends on the one before it. Helicase is step one: mutate it and the helix never opens, no fork forms, and nothing downstream can happen at all.

Q33: What is the role of a primer in DNA replication? What would happen if you forgot to add a primer to a tube containing the reaction mix for a DNA sequencing reaction?

A: The primer provides the free 3′-OH that DNA polymerase needs to start a chain — polymerases cannot initiate synthesis de novo. No primer in the sequencing tube means no starting point: nothing gets copied, no chain-terminated fragments form, and no sequence can be read.

Why: Polymerases cannot start chains from nothing — they can only extend an existing 3′-OH, which is exactly what the primer supplies. In sequencing, every terminated fragment must begin at the same known primer; with no primer there is no first bond, so no fragments, no ladder, and no readable sequence.

Q34: Quinolone antibiotics treat bacterial infections by blocking the activity of topoisomerase. Why does this treatment work?

A: As helicase unwinds the fork, supercoils accumulate ahead of it; bacterial topoisomerase (DNA gyrase) relieves that strain by cutting, swiveling, and re-sealing the DNA. Quinolones block topoisomerase, so torsional strain builds unchecked, the replication fork stalls (transcription stalls too), and stalled machinery colliding with the trapped topoisomerase complexes generates lethal double-strand breaks. Bacteria can't replicate or express their genes, so they die.

Why: Unwinding the helix winds up supercoils ahead of the fork, and gyrase/topoisomerase is the relief valve. Blocking it makes torsional strain stall the fork, and the trapped cleavage complexes become lethal double-strand breaks — replication and transcription both collapse. Targeting a bacterial-specific enzyme also keeps the drug selective.

Q35: How do linear eukaryotic chromosomes ensure that their ends are replicated completely?

A: Telomeres plus telomerase. The last RNA primer on the lagging strand can't be replaced with DNA without leaving a gap, so a bare chromosome end would shorten every round. Telomerase — carrying its own built-in RNA template — extends the 3′ end of the telomere repeats, providing expendable template beyond the coding sequence so the end gets copied completely; the telomere itself is the buffer that can afford to lose a little.

Why: The end-replication problem is that removing the last lagging-strand primer leaves a 5′ gap that no polymerase can fill. Telomerase extends the 3′ end beyond the genes using its built-in RNA template, so the unavoidable gap lands in expendable repeat DNA — the telomere — rather than in coding sequence. The buffer absorbs the loss.

Q36: What is the consequence of mutation of a mismatch repair enzyme? How will this affect the function of a gene?

A: Mismatches that slip past DNA polymerase's proofreading go unrepaired, so errors accumulate with every replication cycle and the mutation rate rises sharply. Any given gene soon carries wrong bases — its protein product loses function (misfolded, truncated, or nonfunctional). In humans, inherited mismatch-repair defects cause Lynch syndrome (hereditary colon cancer predisposition).

Why: Proofreading catches most errors but not all — mismatch repair is the backup layer that fixes what escaped. Without it, errors compound every cycle (a mutator phenotype): genes accumulate wrong bases, their proteins lose function, and in humans this failure mode is Lynch syndrome's inherited cancer predisposition. Redundant repair layers exist because each alone is imperfect.

Q37: An adult with a history of tanning has his genome sequenced; a protein-coding region reads ATGGGGATATGGCAT, while a healthy adult's reads ATGGGGATATGAGCAT. Identify the site and type of mutation.

A: A single-nucleotide deletion — the A at position 12 of the coding region (ATGGGGATATG·A·GCAT → ATGGGGATATGGCAT). Because one base (not a multiple of three) was removed, it is a frameshift: every codon after position 12 is read differently. The tanning history implicates UV-induced pyrimidine dimers as the likely source of the replication error.

Why: Compare the sequences position by position to locate the change: one A is missing at base 12 — and because one base is not a multiple of three, the reading frame of every codon after it shifts (frameshift, not point mutation). The tanning history is the mechanism: UV → pyrimidine dimers → replication errors, the classic route to such deletions.
