## Genetics & Inheritance

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the difference between genotype and phenotype?</strong></summary>

**A:** Genotype = the alleles an organism carries (its genetic makeup, e.g. Rr). Phenotype = the observable traits those alleles produce (e.g. round peas). Genotype determines phenotype, but you can't always read the genotype from the phenotype — a dominant phenotype (round) could be either RR or Rr.

**Why:** The genotype is the instruction set, the phenotype is the expressed result. Dominance masks the distinction: Rr and RR look identical (same phenotype) but breed differently (different genotype), which is exactly why test crosses exist.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What are Mendel's principles?</strong></summary>

**A:**
- **Principle of Segregation** — the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one allele per gene.
- **Principle of Dominance** — in a heterozygote, one allele (dominant) masks the other (recessive); the dominant trait is what shows in the phenotype.
- **Principle of Independent Assortment** — genes for different traits sort into gametes independently of each other (seed color doesn't influence seed shape), which is what produces the 9:3:3:1 dihybrid ratio.

**Why:** Each principle maps to meiosis: homologous chromosomes (and their alleles) separate in anaphase I (segregation), one allele's product can mask the other in a heterozygote (dominance), and different chromosome pairs align and migrate independently in metaphase I (independent assortment). The laws are Mendel's statistics; meiosis is the mechanism.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is a testcross and what is it used for?</strong></summary>

**A:** A testcross crosses an individual with a dominant phenotype but unknown genotype (RR or Rr) against a homozygous recessive individual (rr). The offspring reveal the unknown genotype: all dominant offspring → the parent was RR; a 1:1 dominant:recessive ratio → the parent was Rr.

| | r | r |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R | Rr | Rr |
| r | rr | rr |

**Why:** The homozygous recessive partner can only contribute recessive alleles, so every recessive allele in the unknown parent is exposed — any rr offspring proves the unknown contributed an r and was heterozygous.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the difference between homozygous and heterozygous?</strong></summary>

**A:** Homozygous = both alleles at a gene are the same (RR or rr). Heterozygous = the two alleles differ (Rr). Homozygotes are true-breeding — they produce only one gamete type; heterozygotes produce two.

**Why:** Gametes get one allele per gene, so a homozygote has only one kind to give (RR → all R), while a heterozygote gives either (Rr → half R, half r). That's why only heterozygotes generate the characteristic 3:1 and 1:1 segregation ratios.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the difference between dominant and recessive?</strong></summary>

**A:** A dominant allele is expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present (it masks the other allele in a heterozygote). A recessive allele is only expressed when both copies are recessive (homozygous), because in a heterozygote the dominant allele hides it. Recessive alleles aren't "weaker" — they may encode functional or nonfunctional products that simply aren't seen when a dominant allele is present.

**Why:** Dominance describes the phenotype of the heterozygote, not the strength of the allele. One functional dominant copy is often sufficient for the trait, so the recessive version appears only when no dominant copy is present — masking, not quality.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What are the different forms of inheritance that extend simple dominance?</strong></summary>

**A:**
- **Pleiotropy** — one gene affects several seemingly unrelated traits (e.g. Marfan syndrome affects height, vision, and the heart).
- **Multiple alleles** — a gene has more than two allele versions in the population (ABO system: IA, IB, i), though any individual still carries only two.
- **Epistasis** — one gene's alleles mask or modify another gene's expression (e.g. pigment-deposition gene masking coat color, producing a 9:3:4 ratio in recessive epistasis).
- **Blending inheritance** — the pre-Mendelian (disproved) idea that parental traits mix permanently like fluids. It fails because offspring traits can reappear unchanged in later generations, showing genes are discrete units, not blends.
- **Codominance** — both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote at the same time (IAIB blood type expresses both A and B antigens).
- **Incomplete dominance** — the heterozygote phenotype is intermediate between the two homozygotes (red × white snapdragon → pink), because neither allele fully masks the other.

**Why:** Simple dominance is only one interaction pattern. The others follow from mechanism: a gene product used in many tissues gives many symptoms (pleiotropy), one gene's product can act upstream of another's (epistasis), both products can be displayed (codominance), or partial activity yields an intermediate (incomplete dominance). Blending fails because hereditary units stay discrete and reappear — the core of Mendel's findings.

</details>

**Source:** [OpenStax Biology 2e, Ch. 12](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-introduction) — [§12.1](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability), [§12.2](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits), [§12.3](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-3-laws-of-inheritance); see also [chapter-12.md](./chapter-12.md)

## DNA Structure

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: Describe the structure of DNA (purines vs pyrimidines, complementary, antiparallel, how strands are held together).</strong></summary>

**A:** DNA is a right-handed double helix: two sugar-phosphate backbones on the outside with nitrogenous bases paired inside like rungs. Each nucleotide = deoxyribose sugar + phosphate + one of four bases (A, T, G, C).
- **Purines vs pyrimidines:** Purines (adenine, guanine) are double-ring bases; pyrimidines (cytosine, thymine) are single-ring bases. Each base pair is one purine + one pyrimidine, keeping the helix a constant width.
- **Complementary:** A pairs with T (two hydrogen bonds) and G pairs with C (three hydrogen bonds), so each strand specifies the sequence of the other.
- **Antiparallel:** The two strands run in opposite directions — one 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′ — defined by the sugar carbons the backbone connects (5′-phosphate end vs 3′-OH end).
- **How strands are held together:** Hydrogen bonds between paired bases (2 for A–T, 3 for G–C) plus base-stacking interactions along the helix axis.

**Why:** Purine + pyrimidine pairing keeps every rung the same width (two purines would bulge, two pyrimidines would pinch). Complementarity plus antiparallel orientation means each strand carries the full information to rebuild its partner — the structural basis of replication and transcription. Hydrogen bonds are individually weak but numerous, so the helix is stable yet unzippable.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What are Chargaff's rules?</strong></summary>

**A:** In double-stranded DNA from any species: A = T and G = C (so %A = %T, %G = %C). Base *composition* varies between species (some genomes A+T-rich, others G+C-rich), showing DNA is not a fixed repeating unit. Example: if DNA is 27% A, it is 27% T, leaving 46% split evenly → 23% G and 23% C.

**Why:** Because A pairs only with T and G only with C, their counts must match in any double helix. The between-species variation disproved the tetranucleotide (fixed GACT repeat) hypothesis and pointed to base sequence — not a repeating pattern — as the information.

</details>

**Source:** [OpenStax Biology 2e, §14.2 DNA Structure and Sequencing](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-2-dna-structure-and-sequencing); see also [chapter-14.md](./chapter-14.md)

## DNA Replication

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the semiconservative method of DNA replication?</strong></summary>

**A:** Each new DNA double helix contains one original (parental) strand and one newly synthesized strand — the helix "half conserves" its old material. Each parental strand serves as a template for building its complement, so the two daughter molecules are identical to the parent.

**Why:** Complementarity means each strand fully specifies the other, so splitting the helix and templating a complement is the natural copying mechanism. Meselson and Stahl confirmed it with ¹⁵N/¹⁴N density labeling: after one round all DNA was intermediate density (one heavy + one light strand), ruling out conservative and dispersive models.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What are the roles of the key enzymes in DNA replication, transcription, and translation?</strong></summary>

**A:**

| Enzyme | Process | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **DNA helicase** | Replication | Unwinds the double helix by breaking hydrogen bonds between base pairs, creating the replication fork. |
| **DNA gyrase** (topoisomerase) | Replication | Relieves supercoiling/torsional strain ahead of the fork by cutting, swiveling, and resealing DNA. |
| **DNA primase** | Replication | Lays down a short RNA primer to provide the free 3′-OH that DNA polymerase needs to start. |
| **DNA polymerase** | Replication | Adds nucleotides 5′→3′ complementary to the template; also proofreads (3′→5′ exonuclease). In prokaryotes Pol III is the main replicator, Pol I replaces RNA primers with DNA. |
| **DNA ligase** | Replication | Seals nicks by catalyzing the phosphodiester bond between a 3′-OH and a 5′-phosphate (joins Okazaki fragments). |
| **DNA endonuclease** | Replication/repair | Cuts a DNA strand at an *internal* position (e.g. excising damaged segments like thymine dimers in nucleotide excision repair). |
| **DNA exonuclease** | Replication/repair | Removes nucleotides from the *end* of a strand (e.g. proofreading by DNA pol, removing RNA primers). |
| **RNA polymerase** | Transcription | Synthesizes RNA 5′→3′ from a DNA template; does not require a primer. |
| **Ribosome (peptidyl transferase, rRNA)** | Translation | Catalyzes peptide-bond formation between amino acids as it reads mRNA codons. |

**Why:** Replication is an assembly line with one specialist per step — open (helicase), relieve strain (gyrase), prime (primase), build/proofread (polymerase), remove ends/internal damage (exonuclease/endonuclease), seal (ligase). Exam questions usually name the symptom: unjoined fragments → ligase; supercoiled stuck fork → gyrase; no 3′-OH start → primase; persistent mismatches → proofreading/exonuclease.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: In which direction are nucleotides added to the growing DNA strand?</strong></summary>

**A:** 5′ → 3′ — DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to the free 3′-OH end of an existing strand. The incoming nucleotide's 5′-phosphate bonds to the growing chain's 3′-OH.

**Why:** Polymerization chemistry is one-way: the bond energy comes from the incoming nucleotide's triphosphate and always forms at the chain's 3′-OH. This single constraint is what forces lagging-strand synthesis to proceed in discontinuous fragments.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What are Okazaki fragments?</strong></summary>

**A:** Short stretches of newly synthesized DNA on the lagging strand, each starting with its own RNA primer. As the fork opens, the lagging strand must be built in short 5′→3′ bursts away from the fork; DNA pol I later replaces the primers with DNA and ligase seals the fragments into one continuous strand.

**Why:** DNA polymerase synthesizes only 5′→3′, but the two template strands are antiparallel — so the leading strand can follow the fork continuously, while the lagging strand's template runs the wrong way and must be copied in repeated restarts as more template is exposed.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the role of an RNA primer?</strong></summary>

**A:** A short RNA stretch (about 5–10 nucleotides) laid down by primase, complementary to the template, that provides the free 3′-OH DNA polymerase requires to begin adding nucleotides — polymerases cannot start synthesis de novo.

**Why:** DNA polymerase can only extend an existing chain because forming the first phosphodiester bond needs a 3′-OH to attach to. The RNA primer supplies that starting point and is later removed and replaced with DNA.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the purpose of having multiple origins of replication?</strong></summary>

**A:** Eukaryotic genomes are very large and chromosomes are linear, so a single origin would take far too long to copy. Multiple origins fire simultaneously along each chromosome, creating many replication forks that expand and merge, allowing the whole genome to be replicated in hours instead of weeks.

**Why:** Replication speed per fork is limited by enzyme chemistry, so total time = genome size ÷ number of forks. Eukaryotes gain speed through parallelism; prokaryotes, with a small circular chromosome, can manage with a single origin.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What are thymine dimers?</strong></summary>

**A:** A covalent linkage between two adjacent thymine bases on the same DNA strand, typically caused by ultraviolet (UV) light. The dimer kinks the helix so polymerases cannot read past it, stalling replication and transcription. Cells usually repair them by nucleotide excision repair: enzymes cut out the damaged segment, DNA polymerase fills the gap, and ligase seals it.

**Why:** UV energy fuses neighboring thymines into a cyclobutane dimer, distorting the backbone so the template no longer fits a polymerase's active site. It's physical template damage (not a copying mistake), so it requires excision repair — and unrepaired dimers are a major cause of UV-induced mutations and skin cancer (e.g. xeroderma pigmentosum).

</details>

**Source:** [OpenStax Biology 2e](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-introduction) — [§14.2](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-2-dna-structure-and-sequencing), [§14.3](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-3-basics-of-dna-replication), [§14.4](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-4-dna-replication-in-prokaryotes), [§14.5](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-5-dna-replication-in-eukaryotes), [§14.6](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-6-dna-repair); see also [chapter-14.md](./chapter-14.md)

## Transcription & Translation

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the difference between replication, transcription, and translation?</strong></summary>

**A:**
- **Replication** — copies the entire DNA molecule before cell division (DNA → DNA).
- **Transcription** — copies one gene's DNA sequence into RNA (DNA → RNA), catalyzed by RNA polymerase.
- **Translation** — decodes mRNA into a chain of amino acids (RNA → protein), carried out by ribosomes with tRNAs delivering amino acids.

**Why:** These are the three arrows of information flow, each with its own template, machine, and product: replication duplicates the whole archive, transcription makes a working copy of one gene, and translation builds the functional protein from that copy.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the purpose and process of splicing?</strong></summary>

**A:** Splicing removes introns (noncoding sequences) from eukaryotic pre-mRNA and joins exons (coding sequences) to produce mature mRNA. The spliceosome — a complex of snRNPs (snRNAs + proteins) — recognizes conserved splice signals (GU at the 5′ intron end, AG at the 3′ end, and a branch-point A), cuts the intron out as a lariat, and ligates the exons. Purpose: introns must be removed so the ribosome reads a continuous coding sequence; alternative splicing also lets one gene produce multiple proteins by combining exons in different ways.

**Why:** Eukaryotic genes are interrupted, so the primary transcript is a draft that must be edited before translation. Because exon boundaries define the codon reading frame, splicing must be precise — errors shift the frame and usually wreck the protein — while controlled alternative splicing expands protein diversity from a single gene.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: Where do replication, transcription, and translation take place?</strong></summary>

**A:**
- **Eukaryotes:** Replication — nucleus (also mitochondria/chloroplasts); Transcription — nucleus; Translation — cytoplasm (on free or ER-bound ribosomes). RNA is capped, spliced, and polyadenylated in the nucleus before export.
- **Prokaryotes:** All three occur in the cytoplasm (no nucleus), and transcription and translation are coupled — ribosomes can begin translating an mRNA while it is still being transcribed.

**Why:** A nuclear membrane separates DNA and ribosomes in eukaryotes, creating a mandatory processing interval (regulation and complexity, at the cost of speed). Prokaryotes lack that barrier, so they trade processing complexity for rapid, coupled gene expression.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What are the roles of each type of RNA (mRNA, tRNA, snRNA, rRNA, miRNA)?</strong></summary>

**A:**
- **mRNA (messenger RNA)** — carries protein-coding information from DNA to the ribosome; its codons specify amino acids.
- **tRNA (transfer RNA)** — adapter molecule: one end carries a specific amino acid, the other has an anticodon that base-pairs with the mRNA codon.
- **rRNA (ribosomal RNA)** — structural and catalytic component of ribosomes; the large-subunit rRNA (peptidyl transferase) forms peptide bonds.
- **snRNA (small nuclear RNA)** — RNA component of snRNPs in the spliceosome that recognizes splice sites and helps remove introns from pre-mRNA.
- **miRNA (microRNA)** — short (~21–24 nt) regulatory RNAs that bind complementary mRNA sequences (often with RISC) to block translation or promote mRNA degradation.

**Why:** Gene expression uses different RNAs for different jobs: mRNA is the message, tRNA translates nucleotide code into amino acids, rRNA is both scaffold and catalyst, snRNA edits the transcript, and miRNA tunes how much of that message is ultimately used.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the difference between codons, introns, and exons?</strong></summary>

**A:** **Codon** — a three-nucleotide sequence in mRNA that specifies one amino acid or a stop signal. **Exon** — a coding (expressed) sequence of a eukaryotic gene that remains in mature mRNA and is translated. **Intron** — a noncoding intervening sequence transcribed into pre-mRNA but removed by splicing before translation.

**Why:** Introns and exons are segments of the gene/transcript (what gets kept vs cut out), while codons are the three-base reading units in the finished mRNA. Introns are removed precisely so the remaining exons form a continuous, in-frame set of codons.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the central dogma of biology?</strong></summary>

**A:** Genetic information generally flows DNA → RNA → protein (genes specify mRNA sequences, which specify protein sequences). Classic exception: retroviruses use reverse transcriptase to copy RNA back into DNA (RNA → DNA).

**Why:** The dogma states which molecules specify which: DNA stores information, RNA carries a working copy, and proteins are the functional products — information does not flow backward from protein to nucleic acid. Reverse transcription is the well-known exception to the usual direction.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: How do eukaryotic and prokaryotic processing of genetic information differ?</strong></summary>

**A:**
- **Prokaryotes:** No nucleus; transcription and translation are coupled in the cytoplasm. mRNA is used with little or no processing (typically no introns, no 5′ cap, no poly-A tail).
- **Eukaryotes:** Transcription in the nucleus produces pre-mRNA that must be processed — 5′ 7-methylguanosine cap, intron removal by the spliceosome, and 3′ poly-A tail — before export to the cytoplasm for translation.

**Why:** The nuclear envelope separates transcription from translation in eukaryotes, creating space for RNA processing that adds regulation and transcript diversity (e.g. alternative splicing). Prokaryotes bypass that stage for speed and immediacy.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is a spliceosome? What is its purpose?</strong></summary>

**A:** A large ribonucleoprotein complex in the eukaryotic nucleus, made of snRNPs (snRNAs + proteins), that removes introns from pre-mRNA and ligates exons together. It recognizes splice signals (GU at 5′, branch-point A, AG at 3′), excises the intron as a lariat, and joins the flanking exons.

**Why:** Splicing must be accurate to the nucleotide because exon boundaries set the reading frame. snRNAs help recognize the correct sites by base-pairing, while the complex catalyzes cutting and joining — essential for producing a readable mature mRNA and enabling alternative splicing.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the purpose of the three sites in a ribosome (A, P, E)?</strong></summary>

**A:** During elongation, tRNAs move through three sites:
- **A site (aminoacyl)** — entry point for an incoming charged tRNA whose anticodon matches the current mRNA codon.
- **P site (peptidyl)** — holds the tRNA carrying the growing polypeptide chain; the peptide bond transfers the chain onto the amino acid in the A site.
- **E site (exit)** — holds the now-uncharged tRNA briefly before it leaves the ribosome. After each peptide bond the ribosome translocates one codon, moving tRNAs A → P → E.

**Why:** Translation is stepwise assembly: deliver the next amino acid (A), add it to the chain (P, via peptidyl transferase), release the spent tRNA (E). The sites explain drug effects too — e.g. tetracycline blocks the A site, chloramphenicol blocks peptidyl transfer.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q: What is the difference between the coding strand and the template strand of DNA?</strong></summary>

**A:** The **template strand** is the DNA strand RNA polymerase reads; mRNA is synthesized complementary and antiparallel to it (5′→3′). The **coding strand** (nontemplate strand) is not read, but its sequence matches the mRNA except that DNA has T where RNA has U. Either DNA strand can serve as the template for a given gene.

**Why:** RNA polymerase can only read one strand in one direction, producing an RNA complement of that template — so the other strand ends up looking like the transcript. "Template" names what is read; "coding" names the strand whose sequence spells the codons (with T↔U). Shortcut: copy the coding strand and replace T with U to get the mRNA.

</details>
