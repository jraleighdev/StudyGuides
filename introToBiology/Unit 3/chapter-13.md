## Modern understanding of inheritance

Q: In a test cross for two characteristics, can the predicted frequency of recombinant offspring be 60 percent? Why or why not?

A: No. Recombinant frequency only ranges from 0% (completely linked genes — no crossing over between them) to 50% (unlinked genes — on different chromosomes, or so far apart on the same chromosome that a crossover occurs between them in every meiotic event). 60% is impossible; if it looks like 60%, the two most frequent classes were misidentified — the most frequent offspring are always the parental types.

Why: Recombination frequency is the fraction of gametes in which a crossover occurred between the two loci, and it caps at 50% — the frequency of independent assortment — because even for distant genes, multiple crossovers within one meiosis can restore the parental combination. A number above 50% is mathematically impossible, so seeing one means the parental (most frequent) classes were mislabeled as recombinants.

Q: Given a Drosophila chromosome 2 linkage map — which statement is true?

A: Recombination of the red/brown eye and long/short aristae alleles occurs more frequently than recombination of the alleles for wing length and body color. Map positions: aristae length 0 cM, body color 48.5, cinnabar eye 57.5, wing length 65.5, brown eye 104.5. The brown-eye and aristae loci are 104.5 cM apart — so far apart they recombine essentially freely (~50%, unlinked) — while wing length and body color are only 65.5 − 48.5 = 17 cM apart. Farther apart = more frequent recombination, so the eye–aristae pair recombines far more often.

Why: A map distance in centimorgans *is* the recombination frequency, and the probability that a crossover lands between two genes grows with the distance between them — more DNA between them means more crossover opportunities. So the 104.5 cM eye–aristae gap recombines far more often than the 17 cM wing–body gap, and distance on the map directly predicts recombination rate.

Q: Which statement about nondisjunction is true?

A: Nondisjunction occurring during meiosis II results in 50 percent normal gametes. When sister chromatids fail to separate in one of the two meiosis II divisions, the four gametes are: two normal (n), one n+1, one n−1. By contrast, meiosis I nondisjunction (homologs failing to separate) produces zero normal gametes — all four are abnormal (two n+1, two n−1).

Why: In meiosis II nondisjunction only one of the two cells entering meiosis II carries the stuck sister-chromatid pair — its division yields one n+1 and one n−1 gamete while the other cell divides normally, so half the gametes escape. Meiosis I nondisjunction corrupts both daughter lineages, so all four gametes are abnormal. Which division failed is read from the fraction of normal gametes.

### Chapter 13 Review Questions (OpenStax Biology 2e)

Q4: X-linked recessive traits in humans (or Drosophila) are observed _____.

A: In more males than females — males have only one X, so a single recessive allele is expressed with no second copy to mask it; females need two.

Why: Males are hemizygous for X-linked genes — their single X has no second allele to mask a recessive one, so it's always expressed. Females need the recessive allele on both X's to show the trait; a single copy is masked by the dominant allele.

Q5: The first suggestion that chromosomes may physically exchange segments came from the microscopic identification of _____.

A: Chiasmata — the points where chromatids contact each other during synapsis; Janssen observed them and proposed segments were exchanged there.

Why: Chiasmata — the visible X-shaped contact points where homologous chromatids touch during synapsis — are the physical footprint of crossing over. Janssen's inference: if chromosomes visibly intertwine at these points, segments may be exchanged there — the first hint of recombination.

Q6: Which recombination frequency corresponds to independent assortment and the absence of linkage?

A: 0.50 — genes on different chromosomes (or far apart on the same one) recombine half the time, matching Mendel's independent assortment.

Why: Genes on different chromosome pairs sort randomly in meiosis I, so 50% of gametes are recombinant — recombination frequency 0.5 is independent assortment restated chromosomally, which is the definition of unlinked.

Q7: Which recombination frequency corresponds to perfect linkage and violates independent assortment?

A: 0 — completely linked genes are always inherited together, so no recombinants appear.

Why: Perfect linkage means zero crossovers occur between the genes, so only parental-type gametes are ever made. No recombinants (0%) directly violates independent assortment, which predicts 50% recombinants.

Q8: Which code describes position 12 on the long arm of chromosome 13?

A: 13q12 — chromosome number, then arm (q = long, p = short), then region/band number.

Why: Cytogenetic notation is systematic: chromosome number first, then p (petite = short arm) or q (the long arm), then region and band numbers counting outward from the centromere. Position 12 on chromosome 13's long arm decodes as 13q12.

Q9: In agriculture, polyploid crops (like coffee, strawberries, or bananas) tend to produce _____.

A: Larger yields — polyploid plants tend to be larger and more robust than diploids of their species.

Why: Polyploidy multiplies gene dosage, and extra gene copies often mean more gene product, larger cells, and more vigorous growth — which is why many crops (banana, strawberry, coffee, wheat) are polyploid and valued for larger fruit and yield.

Q10: A pericentric inversion occurred in one of two homologs; the other is normal. What structure would they assume during meiosis to pair accurately along their lengths?

A: A loop — the inverted homolog forms an inversion loop and the normal one molds around it, keeping genes aligned point-for-point during synapsis.

Why: Synapsis aligns homologous loci gene-for-gene; the only way an inverted segment can line up its genes with a normal homolog's is to loop out of the linear axis so each gene faces its partner. The loop is the geometric solution to pairing a reversed sequence with a normal one.

Q11: The genotype XXY corresponds to _____.

A: Klinefelter syndrome — male with an extra X (small testes, reduced fertility). Turner is XO, Jacob is XYY, Triplo-X is XXX.

Why: Sex-chromosome aneuploidies have named syndromes by karyotype: XXY = Klinefelter, XO = Turner, XYY = Jacob, XXX = triple X. The Y chromosome's presence determines maleness regardless of how many X's accompany it.

Q12: Abnormalities in the number of X chromosomes tend to have milder phenotypic effects than the same abnormalities in autosomes because of _____.

A: X inactivation — extra (or missing) X chromosomes are largely silenced as Barr bodies, so dosage is mostly corrected; autosomes have no such mechanism.

Why: Dosage compensation: mammalian cells silence all but one X per cell as Barr bodies, so extra or missing X's have far milder effects than an equivalent autosomal imbalance — autosomes have no inactivation mechanism, and autosomal aneuploidies are often lethal.

Q13: By definition, a pericentric inversion includes the _____.

A: Centromere — peri = around it; paracentric inversions occur outside the centromere.

Why: The prefixes carry the definition: peri- = around/encompassing (a pericentric inversion spans and therefore includes the centromere), while para- = alongside/beside (a paracentric inversion stays within one arm, outside the centromere).

### Chapter 13 Critical Thinking Questions (OpenStax Biology 2e)

Q14: Explain how the Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance helped to advance our understanding of genetics.

A: It proposed that genes reside on chromosomes — giving Mendel's abstract "factors" a physical location. Understanding that chromosomes are linear arrays of genes explained linkage (genes on the same chromosome are inherited together), and crossing over explained recombination (why linked genes sometimes come apart). It also grounded Mendel's laws in meiosis: homolog pairs segregating mirrors the law of segregation, and different pairs sorting independently mirrors independent assortment. This made it possible to map gene order and distance on chromosomes (Sturtevant's linkage maps).

Why: Mendel's laws described patterns without physical units; putting genes on chromosomes explained both the patterns and their exceptions — genes on the same chromosome travel together (linkage), crossing over between them breaks that (recombination), and meiosis mechanics generate Mendel's ratios. A physical basis is also what made gene mapping possible: recombination frequencies became measurable distances.

Q15: Using diagrams, illustrate how nondisjunction can result in an aneuploid zygote.

A: Nondisjunction = homologs (meiosis I) or sister chromatids (meiosis II) failing to separate, producing gametes with the wrong chromosome number. Fertilization by a normal gamete then makes an aneuploid zygote:

- Meiosis I nondisjunction (homologs stay together): all four gametes abnormal — two carry n+1, two carry n−1. An n+1 gamete + normal n gamete → 2n+1 zygote (trisomy); an n−1 gamete + normal n → 2n−1 zygote (monosomy).
- Meiosis II nondisjunction (sister chromatids stay together): two normal n gametes, one n+1, one n−1 — so half the gametes are unaffected, but the n+1/n−1 ones still yield trisomic or monosomic zygotes as above.

(For a diagram: draw the meiosis I nondisjunction showing both homologs migrating to the same pole in anaphase I, then the resulting four gamete chromosome counts; label the fertilization n+1 × n = 2n+1 and n−1 × n = 2n−1.)

Why: Nondisjunction produces gametes carrying n+1 or n−1 chromosomes; joining such a gamete with a normal n gamete gives 2n+1 (trisomy) or 2n−1 (monosomy) — aneuploidy, a whole-chromosome count error rather than a gene mutation. The zygote's abnormality is the arithmetic sum of its parents' gamete counts.
